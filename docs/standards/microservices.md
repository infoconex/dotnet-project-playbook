# STD-017 — Microservices Architecture

Microsoft's [microservices architecture style](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/architecture-styles/microservices) and [service-boundary guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/microservices/model/microservice-boundaries) are the technical starting points. This standard makes a **distinct, conditional architectural decision**: a system that adopts Microservices Architecture must demonstrate meaningful **independent service deployment and operational autonomy**, and must assess the new distributed risks against evidence. It is **not** a universal instruction to distribute an application, nor a new rule for business-policy dependency direction or domain modeling.

This is the **sole normative owner** of STD-017. The [standards catalog](README.md) records identity and level, [governance](../governance/standards.md) controls alternatives, and the [on-demand guide](../microservices-guidance.md) supplies decision scenarios and illustrative verification approaches without adding requirements.

## <a name="std-017"></a>Purpose, level, and applicability

**Level: Recommended (conditional).** Consider Microservices Architecture when a new or materially changed system has **demonstrated value** in independently deploying or operating cohesive responsibilities—for example, different release cadences or ownership, genuinely distinct scaling/failure-containment requirements, or material isolation needs—and the organization has sufficient operational capability to sustain it. The value should outweigh the additional complexity of network calls, independently evolving contracts, cross-service consistency, multiple deployments, security boundaries, and observability.

**Adoption is a choice, not a default.** Prefer a monolith, modular monolith, or proportionate single-project design when independent deployments add little value, consistency benefits from local transactions, a cohesive team can operate one application effectively, or distributed operations would be disproportionate. This includes pure libraries, many CRUD systems, and business-logic-heavy applications whose capabilities do not need separate operations. An application with several endpoints, features, teams, bounded contexts, compiled projects, processes, or containers does **not** thereby adopt microservices.

**Conditional adoption:** If a project explicitly selects Microservices Architecture for a system or a defined subsystem, assess that scope against the adopted-style expectations below. Record a deliberate simpler alternative and its relevant tradeoff where architecture selection is material. Omission of microservices does not violate STD-017 and does not waive independently Required standards. For an existing implementation claiming microservices, distinguish the intended architecture from the operational autonomy that is actually implemented and verified.

The principles apply across supported modern .NET and traditional .NET Framework environments, including on-premises and hybrid deployment, but actual compiler, language, hosting, operating-system, testing, and deployment capabilities must be established for the **specific** application. A legacy ASP.NET or .NET Framework workload is not presumed to support modern .NET hosting APIs or cross-platform deployment.

## Normative expectations when adopted

1. **Define cohesive service responsibility and ownership.** Identify the business responsibility each meaningful service owns, its source of authoritative business decisions and invariants, its consumers, its data authority, and the party accountable for changing and operating it. Evaluate real change coupling, domain boundaries, scaling, availability, regulatory constraints, and organization. A bounded context may inform a service boundary but is not necessarily a one-to-one deployable service. Avoid technical-layer decomposition, service-per-endpoint/feature/team quotas, tiny chatty services, and unexplained mutual runtime or release dependencies. Reconsider boundaries when coordinated changes or dependency chains defeat autonomy.

2. **Preserve real independent release capability.** Define a discoverable service deployment unit and a practical way to build, release, advance, and—where needed—roll back or roll forward that service without routinely requiring simultaneous redeployment of its consumers or providers. Use compatible service contracts, deliberate schema evolution, and safe transition periods. Name material dependencies on other releases and assess coupling honestly: routinely synchronized releases or a jointly mutable data schema can indicate a **distributed monolith**, even when components run in separate processes. Shared source repositories, CI/CD infrastructure, infrastructure provisioning, deployment tooling, or operational platforms are permitted when service-level release choice remains genuine. There is no requirement for one pipeline, repository, dedicated development team, or Kubernetes resource per service.

3. **Protect service-owned business and data authority.** Each service controls authoritative updates to the business state and persistence it owns; other services must not bypass the owner and independently mutate its tables, repositories, schema, or state. Use explicit owner-controlled interaction contracts for material cross-service access, including appropriate published read access, exports, projections, or integration mechanisms. Shared **physical database servers** and other infrastructure are permitted with genuine ownership and access isolation; uncontrolled sharing of authoritative schemas/tables is not. Define relevant read and write authority, access permissions, consistency boundaries, integrity/concurrency guarantees, and reporting paths. Preserve independently Required [business-policy independence (STD-011)](dependency-direction.md#std-011) and adopted [DDD ownership (STD-014)](ddd.md#std-014).

4. **Make distributed consistency explicit where it matters.** Identify business outcomes needing atomic consistency, those tolerating stale or eventually consistent views, and partially completed cross-service workflows. Keep a critical invariant within one service transaction when that is the simplest valid guarantee. Where a workflow genuinely spans service-owned transactions, define outcome coordination, retries and duplicates, recovery/reconciliation, and compensation or human intervention for meaningful failures. Choose local transactions, synchronous owner operations, durable messaging, sagas, outbox/inbox, idempotency records, projections, or other patterns **only if warranted**. Do not mandate distributed transactions, eventual consistency for all data, a broker, event sourcing, or CQRS. A compensation action may not undo an irreversible business effect; state the remaining risk.

5. **Design compatible communication and consequential failure behavior.** Specify important consumer/provider request and response or message contracts, expected errors, semantic versions/compatibility, and a migration strategy for breaking changes. Use HTTP APIs, gRPC, or asynchronous messaging based on latency, availability, workload, temporal coupling, and genuine fan-out needs. Distinguish internal domain events from externally published integration contracts when events are used. For significant network boundaries, address bounded timeouts, cancellation, retry safety and idempotency, concurrency, partial outages, overload/backpressure, and message ordering or duplicate delivery **where applicable**. Select rate limiting, circuit breakers, bulkheads, service discovery, queues, or a mesh only when needed. Avoid long synchronous chains where an unavailable dependency blocks unrelated work.

6. **Secure the actual trust boundaries.** Identify users, service principals/workload identities, tenants, entry points, transport paths, data ownership, and privileged operations. Apply authentication, appropriate **service-level authorization**, least privilege, protected runtime/build credentials, secure transport, tenant isolation where relevant, API exposure controls, audit/security logging and deployment/supply-chain controls according to actual risks. Assess asynchronous publishers and consumers as trust boundaries too, including event authenticity, permissions, and sensitive payload handling. Internal network membership and gateway authentication alone do not establish authorization when services can be reached through another path. Protect sensitive information in logs, traces, and metrics. A particular Azure product, security library, gateway, or mesh is not prescribed.

7. **Make operations accountable and observable.** Name the owner responsible for deployability, runtime configuration, health, incident response, recovery and service-level change of each material service. Provide operational signals proportionate to importance: meaningful health/availability and error metrics, useful logs, and correlation across material remote or asynchronous workflows. Define service-level objectives and alert/recovery expectations where business impact warrants them; not every trivial service needs an independent formal SLO or 24/7 dedicated team. Monitor rollout health and identify what a failed deployment, network partition, dependency outage, or partially completed business operation means to users. Shared observability and operations infrastructure is valid.

8. **Verify and qualify meaningful architectural claims.** For new or materially changed adopted service boundaries, gather proportional **design, source, contract, test, integration, deployment, and operational evidence** for the properties asserted. Static inspection can identify ownership, references, release definitions, service interfaces, data-access patterns, intended security configuration, and documented recovery; it cannot prove that deployed independent releases, effective authorization, fault tolerance, actual consistency, scalability, or telemetry work. Exercise representative real or appropriately provider-validated external dependencies when the [STD-009](testing.md#std-009) risk trigger applies. Where independence, compatibility, security, recovery, observability, or performance is a consequential claim, use relevant running-system, release, failure/recovery, or load evidence appropriate to the claim, or explicitly report that it remains unverified. No mandatory test framework, service mesh, chaos tool, full production-scale environment, or test for every trivial call is introduced.

The distinctive adopted-style responsibility is **deployment and operational autonomy with accountable distributed boundaries and honest evidence**, not a duplication of other standards' general engineering obligations.

## Logical, compiled, runtime, and deployment boundaries

| Boundary | What it establishes | What it does **not** establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domain model/bounded context | Meaning and ownership of particular business concepts | A one-to-one service, repository, assembly, or process |
| Module or vertical slice | Logical business or feature organization and change locality | A separate deployment or runtime service |
| Compiled project/assembly | Source reference and accessibility enforcement | Independent deployment, scaling, or operations |
| Runtime process/container | Execution and possibly resource/failure isolation | Compatible independent release and business/data authority |
| Deployment unit | A separately installable or replaceable artifact/instance | Service autonomy without contract, data, operational, and release discipline |
| Independently operated service | Cohesive authority and a real independent release/operational lifecycle | Mandatory Azure, containers, Kubernetes, service mesh, or separate repository |

A service may have several assemblies and internally organized features. Shared hosting technology, code repositories, deployment automation, and database **servers** do not invalidate microservices. Conversely, a collection of separately running services whose schema or releases must repeatedly advance in lockstep may be a distributed monolith.

## Applicability examples and alternatives

- **Small library, cohesive application, or CRUD system:** A single project or monolith with sound responsibilities and relevant tests is often better. No fake service, messaging infrastructure, or deployment pipeline is necessary.
- **Business-logic-heavy system with several capabilities:** Evaluate DDD, modules, and slices first. Independently deployed services are justified only by release or operational benefits beyond logical modularity.
- **Independent teams or different scaling/availability profiles:** Microservices are stronger candidates when measured release coordination, resource needs, failure containment, or bounded accountability support the tradeoff. Team count alone does not.
- **Strict regulatory or security constraints:** Assess whether physical/service separation improves the required control and can actually be operated and verified. Distribution can increase the attack surface and is not automatically safer.
- **Existing monolith or modular monolith:** Preserve a stable architecture unless an identified extraction delivers net value. Prefer incremental migration around a coherent responsibility with stable contracts, characterized behavior, and explicit data migration/recovery.
- **Legacy .NET Framework, on-premises, or hybrid systems:** Use supported Windows/MSBuild/runtime and actual hosting facilities where applicable; no blanket rewrite to modern SDK-style .NET or automatic container/cross-OS claim.

A Recommended style permits a justified alternative documented where it is considered. A system **claiming adopted microservices** that lacks contract compatibility, credible independent releases, or data authority should document the deviation and describe its real distributed-monolith risks rather than claiming verified conformity. Transitional exceptions may be planned with a bounded migration and explicit risk; independently Required violations still use their own reviewed exception process.

## Proportionate verification and evidence limits

1. **Selection evidence:** Record the real business/operational driver, plausible monolithic or modular alternatives, expected value, distributed costs, and actual organizational capacity. A folder/repository/service count is not an adoption rationale.
2. **Boundary inspection:** Review business ownership, bounded contexts where relevant, actual source and data paths, public APIs/messages, schema migration dependencies, and release coordination. Static topology and architecture tests support—but cannot replace—semantic ownership review.
3. **Meaningful behavior and integration:** Test representative success/rejection/failure paths through actual service contracts. Use consumer/provider validated contracts or real integration where appropriate; apply STD-001 and STD-009 on their independent triggers. Select cross-service workflow, concurrency, duplicate, consistency, tenant-isolation, and security scenarios according to risk.
4. **Release and deployment evidence:** Where claiming independent deployment, inspect/version actual service artifacts and pipeline configuration **and** verify representative service rollout, rollback/roll-forward or compatible coexistence in a suitable environment. A configured pipeline alone proves only configuration, not a successful independent release.
5. **Distributed operation evidence:** Where material claims are made, exercise or observe representative partial failures, timeouts, recovery, correlation across service boundaries, alert/health behavior, and consequential load/scaling behavior. Test fidelity and environment limits must be stated. Fault injection is useful when risk warrants it, not universally mandatory.
6. **Report what actually happened:** Capture exact revision, target services, toolchains/environments, commands and tests executed, provider/contract versions, results, reviewed dependencies, justified deviations, and remaining unknowns. Distinguish **documented design**, **implemented configuration**, and **observed runtime behavior**. A successful build, passing isolated unit tests, framework feature, or this document's examples alone do not prove reliability, security, scalability, consistency, or independently operable services.

No executable validation or consumer template is added by this documentation-only standard. The [on-demand guide](../microservices-guidance.md#level-3--assess-verify-and-evolve) supplies non-normative scenarios and evidence checklists.
